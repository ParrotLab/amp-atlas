# Reviewing someone else's work

When a teammate submits a Draft and picks you as a reviewer, it's your job to read their
changes and decide: is this ready to become official, or does it need more work? This guide
walks through how to review well.

Reviewing is one of the most valuable things you do in AMP Atlas. It's how your team keeps
its knowledge trustworthy — every review catches something that would otherwise slip
through.

---

## Finding your reviews

Reviews waiting on you show up in your **Inbox**. Open it to see the Drafts teammates have
asked you to look at. Click one to open the **review**.

---

## Reading the changes

Inside a review, you'll see the changes laid out document by document. For each one, you can
see exactly what's different from the current Live Version.

- **See it two ways.** You can look at the **final result** (what the document will read
  like if published) or focus on **just the changes** (what's different). Toggle between them
  to get the full picture.
- **Check things off as you go.** As you finish reviewing each document, mark it
  **✓ Reviewed** so you can keep track of what you've covered — handy when a Draft touches
  several documents.

Take your time. A good review means actually reading the work, not just glancing at it.

---

## The one rule: you can't edit their Draft

This is the most important thing to understand about reviewing:

> **You cannot edit someone else's Draft. Your feedback goes through comments only.**

That might feel limiting at first, but it's deliberate and important. The Draft belongs to
its author. If reviewers could reach in and change it directly, the author would lose track
of their own work, and it would be unclear who actually decided what. Instead, **you tell
the author what you think, and they make the changes.** That keeps ownership clear and keeps
the author in control of their work.

So your tools as a reviewer are **comments** and a **decision** — not editing.

---

## Leaving comments

You can comment in a couple of ways:

- **On a specific spot** — point at a particular line or section you have feedback on.
- **On the whole review** — a general comment about the Draft overall (a summary of your
  thoughts, a big-picture concern, or overall praise).

### Say what *kind* of comment it is

To make your feedback easy to act on, label each comment with what kind it is. This tiny
habit removes a lot of guesswork for the author:

- **blocking** — this must change before the work can be published.
- **nitpick** — a small style or wording preference; nice to fix, but optional.
- **question** — you want to understand something; not a request for a change (yet).
- **praise** — something done well. Worth saying! Reviews aren't only for problems.

When an author sees "**nitpick:** maybe tighten this sentence," they know it's optional. When
they see "**blocking:** this rubric score is wrong," they know it has to be fixed. Clear
labels make the whole cycle faster.

### Review at the right layer

Check what layer of feedback the author asked for (see
[the 3 Layers of Work](./submit-for-review.md#ask-for-the-right-kind-of-feedback-the-3-layers-of-work)).
If they said the structure is still being worked out, focus your comments there — don't
nitpick wording on something whose whole shape might change. Meeting the author at the layer
they asked for makes your review land.

---

## Making your decision

When you've read everything, you make the call:

- **Approve** — the work is good to become official. Approving clears the Draft to be
  **published** to the Live Version.
- **Request Changes** — there's something that needs addressing first. Use this when you've
  left **blocking** comments. The author will make the fixes in their Draft and the review
  updates automatically, so you can take another look.

Approve when it's genuinely ready. Request changes when it's not. Either way, you've done
the job — you've kept the Live Version trustworthy.

---

## A good reviewer's mindset

- **Be specific.** "This section is confusing" is hard to act on; "this step doesn't say who
  does it" is easy.
- **Separate must-fix from nice-to-have.** That's what the labels are for.
- **Say what's good, too.** Praise tells the author what to keep doing.
- **Remember it's not adversarial.** Requesting changes isn't a criticism of the author —
  it's the system working. Catching things together is the whole point.

---

**You should now be able to** find reviews in your Inbox, read the changes both ways, leave
clearly-labeled comments (without editing the author's Draft), review at the layer they
asked for, and decide between **Approve** and **Request Changes**. Next:
