# Reviewing someone else's work

When a teammate submits a Draft and picks you as a reviewer, it's your job to read their
changes and decide: is this ready to become official, or does it need more work? This guide
walks through how to review well.

Reviewing is one of the most valuable things you do in AMP Atlas. It's how your team keeps
its knowledge trustworthy — every review catches something that would otherwise slip through.

---

## Finding your reviews

Reviews waiting on you show up in your **Inbox**, under the **Needs your review** tab. Open
one to see the review.

---

## Reading the changes

Inside a review, the changed files are laid out as a list you can expand. For each file, you
can look at it two ways with a toggle:

- **Updated version** — the file as it will read if published (the finished result).
- **What changed** — just the differences, highlighted, so you can see exactly what's new or
  removed.

As you finish each file, mark it **✓ Reviewed**. You'll need to mark **every** file reviewed
before you can approve — that's AMP Atlas making sure nothing goes out unlooked-at. Take your
time; a good review means actually reading the work, not just glancing at it.

---

## The rule: don't edit someone else's Draft

This is the most important habit in reviewing:

> **Don't edit a teammate's Draft. Give feedback and let *them* make the changes.**

The Draft belongs to its author. If reviewers reached in and changed things directly, authors
would lose track of their own work and it'd be unclear who decided what. So as a reviewer,
your job is to **tell the author what needs to change** — not to fix it yourself. This keeps
ownership clear and keeps the author in control of their work.

> _This one is a team agreement, not something the app forces. Even if you find a way to open
> the author's Draft, the right move is to hand feedback back to them, not edit it._

---

## Leaving your feedback

Your feedback goes in a **single note** in the review's action bar — a short write-up of what
you think and what (if anything) needs to change. Then you make your decision (below).

> **What the review does *not* have (yet):** there's no way to pin a comment to a specific
> line, and there are no threaded back-and-forth comments inside AMP Atlas. It's one note plus
> your decision. If you ever need to point at an exact line for a technical teammate, there's a
> quiet **View on GitHub** link — but for everyday reviews, the note is all you need.

### Writing a note that's easy to act on

Since it's one note, a little structure goes a long way. Two habits your team uses:

- **Say what kind of feedback each point is.** Labeling your points tells the author what's
  essential vs. optional:
  - **blocking** — must change before this can be published.
  - **nitpick** — a small preference; nice to fix, but optional.
  - **question** — you want to understand something; not a change request (yet).
  - **praise** — something done well. Worth saying — reviews aren't only for problems.
- **Focus on the layer the author asked for.** If they said the structure is still in flux,
  give feedback there — don't nitpick wording on something whose shape might change. (See
  [the three layers](./submit-for-review.md#ask-for-the-right-kind-of-feedback).)

For example: *"Structure looks right. **blocking:** the LAUNCH rubric score in step 3 is
wrong. **nitpick:** the intro could be tighter. **praise:** the examples are great."*

> These labels are a **writing habit for your note**, not buttons in the app — you just type
> them. They make the single note far easier for the author to act on.

---

## Making your decision

When you've read everything and written your note, make the call:

- **Approve** — the work is good to become official. A note is optional here. Approving is a
  **sign-off** — it clears the Draft so the *author* can publish it. (Approving does **not**
  publish it yourself.)
- **Request changes** — something needs addressing first. A note is **required** here, so the
  author knows what to fix. Their Draft moves back to them; they make the changes and submit
  again for you to re-check.

Approve when it's genuinely ready. Request changes when it's not. Either way, you've kept the
Live Version trustworthy.

---

## A good reviewer's mindset

- **Be specific.** "This section is confusing" is hard to act on; "step 3 doesn't say who does
  it" is easy.
- **Separate must-fix from nice-to-have.** That's what the labels in your note are for.
- **Say what's good, too.** Praise tells the author what to keep doing.
- **It's not adversarial.** Requesting changes isn't a criticism of the author — it's the
  system working. Catching things together is the whole point.

---

files reviewed, leave one clear, well-structured note (without editing the author's Draft),
and decide between **Approve** and **Request changes**. Next:
